Longer life, less maintenance: NanoBrasil brings nanotechnology to the challenge of Brazil's highways

Oct 14, 2026·NanoBrasil News Agency·3 min read

Making a pavement last longer means changing an equation that involves maintenance, traffic disruptions, material consumption and the efficiency of infrastructure investments. This is the challenge in which NanoBrasil is beginning to apply its nanotechnology experience, in partnership with highway concessionaires, to develop materials capable of making roads and structures stronger, more durable and more efficient.

The economic dimension of this effort is significant. In 2025 alone, Brazil's National Department of Transport Infrastructure (DNIT) invested approximately R$ 8 billion in corrective and routine maintenance services on the roughly 57,000 kilometers of federal highways under its administration. The figure does not include concession networks, but it helps gauge the weight of road maintenance in the country.

The work started by NanoBrasil involves incorporating nanomaterials into pavements, binders, concrete and materials used in soil stabilization. At the nanoscale, changes in the composition of and interaction between materials can modify physicochemical and mechanical properties and open the way to gains in strength, adhesion, durability and curing time.

The work starts precisely from the possibility of getting more performance out of the materials used in infrastructure that is continuously subjected to loads, weather and wear. For concessionaires, gains of this kind can mean fewer interventions over the life cycle of highways, better use of raw materials and greater efficiency of the resources allocated to construction and maintenance.

Roads on another scale

The application of nanotechnology to highways is already the subject of research and demonstration outside Brazil. In the European Union, the DURABROADS project developed asphalts modified with carbon nanomaterials and took the solutions to a pilot section in Spain, in addition to subjecting them to technical, economic and environmental assessments.

Among the results, the project produced mixes that met Spanish specifications for heavier-traffic roads and used large proportions of reused materials. Depending on the formulation, between 65% and 97% of the natural aggregates were replaced by reclaimed asphalt and slag.

In the analyses carried out by the project over a 50-year horizon, all the alternatives compared with their respective base cases showed lower environmental impact and lower life-cycle cost. In other words, working on matter at the nanoscale is already part of the international search for more durable pavements and for infrastructure capable of consuming fewer resources over its service life.

“In this way, nanotechnology is no longer just a tool for improving materials and becomes an innovation platform for the sector, making it possible to develop more durable, efficient, economical and sustainable solutions for the current and future challenges of road infrastructure,” says Manuel Rodríguez, NanoBrasil's Innovation and Development coordinator.

From soil to pavement

Among the possibilities being studied by NanoBrasil is the treatment of low-strength soils. The idea is to assess whether nanomaterials can modify the characteristics of these soils and expand their use in road works, reducing the limitations these materials present in their original condition.

Other lines of work include pavements, binders and concrete, as well as research into raw material consumption and execution and curing times.

“We are working, in partnership with concessionaires, on different fronts for applying nanotechnology to road infrastructure and civil engineering,” explains Manuel. According to him, the development seeks to “optimize materials and processes, reduce operating costs, and increase the performance and service life of structures.”

Entering this field extends the application of NanoBrasil's technology platform to a sector in which small gains in material performance can take on another dimension when multiplied by thousands of kilometers of infrastructure.
